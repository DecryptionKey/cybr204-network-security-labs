# Cognitive Load, Fatigue, and Security Decision-Making

*Literature review written for CYBR204 (University at Albany, February 2026).*

This review traces how research on the human side of security operations has moved from surveying operator stress, to measuring it objectively (eye tracking, digital behavior), to organizational fixes. It covers five papers from 2018 to 2025.

## Background and Related Works

The intersection of human factors and cybersecurity is a critical research area. Most security incidents (about 80-90%) result from human errors, not just technology failures. Scholars shifted from measuring stress in real operations to using objective physiological monitoring. This progress integrated human factors into cybersecurity design and practice. The literature creates a path: first, identifying operator stress; then examining its forms; measuring cognitive impacts with new technologies; and finally, proposing organizational frameworks to reduce these risks.

### Dykstra & Paul (2018) — Measuring Stress in Cyber Operations

Dykstra and Paul were among the first to highlight the human element in cybersecurity operations. They addressed the continuous, understudied issue of operator stress, including fatigue, frustration, and cognitive workload in real-time tactical cyber contexts. Their research noted that although technology resilience is well studied, human resilience is not, even though it is crucial for cybersecurity effectiveness. The study focused on tactical cyber operations. These require swift, precise actions, where prolonged activity increases the risk of network detection, and specialized staff are costly to train and retain. The main contribution was the Cyber Operations Stress Survey (COSS), a cost-effective tool. It combines pre- and post-operational measures with validated NASA Task Load Index (TLX) factors to assess cognitive stress. Results from four NSA location case studies with 126 participants indicated a 16% increase in operator fatigue and a 12% increase in frustration during operations. The average cognitive workload score (RTLX) was 44.55. COSS provided valuable subjective data, but the authors noted its limitations in explaining the causes of operator stress. They suggested adding objective biometric measures, such as heart rate and eye movements, to detect and predict stress in real time. This approach could enable automated risk assessments that outperform generalized heuristic models. Unresolved issues include: identifying the specific roots of stress in cyber operations, determining how high-stakes environments affect self-assessed performance, and understanding why performance measures correlate in unexpected ways. These questions suggest more research is needed.

### Nobles (2022) — Stress, Burnout, and Security Fatigue as a Human Factors Problem

Building on this foundation, the broader systemic nature of human performance issues in cybersecurity was detailed. A thorough analysis reframed stress, burnout, and security fatigue as basic human factors problems. These require organizational-level intervention. Nobles addressed the critical gap between technology-centric cybersecurity approaches and the reality that human weaknesses remain the main attack vector. The research's importance is clear in its documentation of the increasing costs of neglecting human factors. CISOs and security professionals now face unprecedented levels of stress and short tenures. Workplace stress results in large financial losses and lost workdays every year. The paper's key contributions include a detailed taxonomy of human performance issues. It distinguishes between stress, burnout, and various forms of security fatigue, including authentication, cognitive, and decision fatigue. It also identifies a "vicious cycle" in which continuous security changes lead to non-compliance. This prompts new security controls that are often misdiagnosed as technical problems rather than human factors issues. This cycle worsens fatigue and burnout. Most importantly, the work introduced the concept of High-Reliability Cybersecurity Organizations (HRCOs). These are based on mindfulness, responsiveness, and learning capacity as a framework for closing human performance gaps. The research could be extended through empirical studies. These should evaluate HRCO principles in practice across diverse organizations. Further steps include quantitative assessments of human factors interventions and their financial impact. Cross-cultural analyses should examine how these issues appear globally. Unresolved issues remain. What strategies work for organizations with few resources? How can HRCO's effectiveness be shown empirically? What methods best measure the ROI of human factors efforts? How can these solutions last as threats evolve? What adjustments are needed for smaller companies?

### Nepal et al. (2024) — Burnout in Cybersecurity Incident Responders

The move from self-report measures to objective physiological indicators marks a major methodological advance. Nepal and colleagues' mixed-methods study examined burnout in cybersecurity incident responders. It addressed burnout and skilled staff shortages using self-reports and digital records from 35 responders at a large IT company. Incident response roles are very stressful, requiring swift reactions and constant pressure. Over half of the participants (N=19) suffered burnout. The study found burnout-linked behaviors associated with heavier workloads, less control, weak teamwork, and little recognition. Burned-out responders worked more than 40 hours per week, slept poorly, and joined more emails, meetings, and after-hours collaborations. These digital behaviors became objective markers of burnout. The study's organizational recommendations made the findings more useful. Key issues remain. Does digital activity cause burnout, and does this happen in other cybersecurity jobs? Are there other factors, and how can models balance workload and security needs in high-threat environments?

### Bernard et al. (2021) — Cognitive Load in Cybersecurity Training

Advances in cognitive load measurement have created new ways to evaluate human performance in cybersecurity. Bernard and colleagues studied how cognitive load affects learning in cybersecurity education. Complex topics often overload students. Their research aimed to improve instructional design by reducing intrinsic and extraneous cognitive load and boosting germane cognitive load, the effort required for learning. The study stands out for using eye-tracking metrics, like fixation duration and pupil size, to measure cognitive load. This approach avoids the limits of self-reports. The main contribution was a new teaching model. It blends Bloom’s taxonomy with content segmentation and interactivity. The model was tested in a controlled trial with 19 computer science students who learned about integer overflow in a popular programming language. Results from eye-tracking showed that segmented, interactive modules reduced cognitive load compared to traditional methods. Future work can extend this model to additional cybersecurity topics, test long-term retention, explore individual differences, and assess whether eye-tracking works with larger groups. Questions remain. What is the best segmentation for topics? Does lower load improve real-world performance? How can interactivity balance with full coverage in short programs?

### Vaishnav & Goel (2025) — Cognitive Load and Security Alert Responses

Recently, researchers applied cognitive load theory to security decision-making. New studies look at how cognitive load affects responses to security alerts. Vaishnav and Goel addressed the problem of human error, such as slips and lapses, when users handle security warnings under different loads. This is important because alert fatigue and poor responses remain a major organizational weakness. Their research used Cognitive Load Theory and the Theory of Planned Behavior. It studied user actions and response times. Researchers ran a lab experiment with 70 university students. Students completed 40 tasks and reacted to random security alerts. Key findings: Higher cognitive load led to more ignored and negative alert responses. Lower cognitive load led to faster, more accurate responses. This shows the cognitive causes of security warning failures. Future research could add planned gaze and micro-expression analysis to classify user decisions. Other steps include studying the effects of different cognitive load types and whether training improves alertness under high load. Open questions remain. Do lab results hold in real workplaces? At what cognitive load level does decision-making begin to fail, and how can this be measured? Can alert design reduce the effects of high load? Do personal differences, such as memory capacity, affect these results?

## Conclusion

Collectively, these works establish a thorough research arc that moves from recognizing stress as a problem in cybersecurity operations to gauging it through both subjective and objective means, understanding its cognitive mechanisms through advanced technologies, and proposing systemic organizational solutions. The progression reveals several key themes: first, the inadequacy of purely technological approaches to cybersecurity that neglect human factors; second, the value of combining subjective self-report measures with objective physiological and behavioral data; third, the critical role of cognitive load in both learning and operational security contexts; and fourth, the need for organizational-level interventions rather than individual-focused solutions. However, significant gaps remain in translating research findings into practical, scalable interventions, particularly for resource-constrained organizations. Further gaps exist in understanding the long-term effectiveness of proposed solutions amid ever-evolving cyber threats and changing work environments. The integration of real-time physiological monitoring, predictive analytics for burnout and stress, and human-centered security design principles represents the forefront of this research domain, with implications that extend beyond cybersecurity into any high-stress, high-consequence operational environment where human performance is detrimental to mission success.
